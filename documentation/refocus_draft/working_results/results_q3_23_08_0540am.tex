helps direct height prediction, which model architecture performs best, and whether adding
Chapman-Richards physics guidance to a neural network helps further.

\paragraph*{XGBoost has the highest predictive accuracy}, ($R^2 = 0.674$), ahead of the standard
deep neural network (DNN, $0.655$) and both physics-informed variants (PINN: $0.631$; PINN-$k$:
$0.618$; Table~\ref{tab:results-q3}). XGBoost wins every fold on the 6-survey cohort
(Appendix~\ref{app:results-q3-6survey}) and loses just 1 of 5 folds on the 4-survey data, by
only $0.007$ $R^2$. The 6-survey cohort is narrower by construction, though -- only 48
compartments and 13,897 plots, against 4-survey's 296 compartments and 71,766 plots, with
high-elevation terrain underrepresented because requiring six clean survey years filters out
the plots least consistently tracked over time; the rest of this chapter continues on 4-survey
only, for its wider environmental range. We checked four possible reasons for the DNN-to-XGBoost
gap, and ruled out all four: feature interactions (DNN captures 80\% of the nonlinear gain over
the linear baseline, despite XGBoost placing 27\% of split weight on interactions), training
set size (subsampling compartments did not widen the gap), network size (sweeping five
architectures gave no real gain), and unfamiliar terrain (XGBoost actually degraded more than
DNN on the 4.1\% of plots with out-of-range slopes and elevations, with error ratios of $1.312$
vs.\ $1.243$). Rerunning training with verified batch sizes gave the same result. The real
question for Q3 is whether PINNs earn their place through safety and interpretability, despite
this lower score.

% --- 2b. The thing driving it is real (environment sweep) -- unchanged ---

\paragraph*{Environment helps a lot -- once. More does not help further.}
Tested PINN with no environment information, then three feature lists, small to large. Five-
fold CV throughout (Table~\ref{tab:pinn-conditions}, Figure~\ref{fig:env-sweep}).
Zero environment info to a small list: a real, solid jump. Paired fold by fold (no-environment
and Set2 share the same five splits), Set2 beats no-environment in all five folds for both
model versions, no exceptions.
Error bars differ in size because two folds are consistently harder than the rest for every
condition, not just no-environment -- likely which compartments happen to fall in those folds,
not a feature-set effect.
Once any environment is present, PINN-$k$ is more consistent fold to fold than plain PINN
(smaller error bars in Set2/Set3/Set4). Without environment, that gap disappears -- the two
versions are equally variable, both essentially reduced to a constant.
The two no-environment points sit close together (0.572 vs 0.575) but not identical -- only one
of the five folds landed on the exact same score for both versions; the rest differ slightly,
since the shared trunk network still trains independently even when neither version can
personalise.

\paragraph*{The over-productive mismatch has a structure, not just noise -- but the reason is
narrower than first thought.}
The two compartments disagreeing with yield class (2057, 1027) are both old (60, 72 years) and
wind-exposed; the three agreeing ones are both young (25--35 years) and sheltered (full
breakdown: Table~\ref{tab:productivity-top5}). Wind exposure still stands as a real explanation
-- the single biggest terrain input to the model (Figure~\ref{fig:terrain-importance}, 15.2\%).
Age-extrapolation does not: it reversed once Table~\ref{tab:age-height-error} was pooled across
all 5 folds (PINN variants are now comparatively BETTER at old ages, not worse), so why age
tracks the mismatch here is now an open question, not an explained one. 2057 is also PINN-$k$'s
own top compartment for growth rate pooled across all 5 folds (rank 1/178) -- two models, two
parameters, converging on the same unusual compartment regardless of the age question. Caveat:
only 5 compartments checked.

\paragraph*{The weakness at extremes is real, but narrower than it first looked.}
Checked whether errors cluster by age or height, pooled across all 5 folds
(Table~\ref{tab:age-height-error}). Shortest stands (0--10m) hold up as fold-0 suggested: DNN
clearly best (MAE 2.68m vs.\ 3.37m plain PINN, 3.15m PINN-$k$) -- ties to compartment 1129's
implausible cases, a personal curve fit mostly to well-observed ranges not stretching well to
unfamiliar territory. Oldest ages (75--93yr) reverse once pooled: DNN is now the WORST of the
three (6.83m vs.\ 6.40m plain PINN), not the best -- not a symmetric ``both extremes'' story any
more; at the old-age extreme, PINN's curve structure is now an advantage over DNN's unconstrained
fit. Caveat: the oldest-age band is the smallest ($n$=751), so treat the reversal with real
uncertainty. This matters beyond height accuracy alone: forestry's volume formula uses height at
a power of 1.48--1.69, so a 10\% height error compounds into roughly 15--17\% volume error, and
lands on the shortest, youngest stands specifically -- not the oldest ones the fold-0-only
reading had pointed to.

% 3. PLAIN PINN VS. PINN-K: THE RECOMMENDATION -- gains a new safety point.

\paragraph*{The simpler PINN wins on accuracy. The two-parameter version buys a fuller,
and safer, picture.}
Compared the two versions everywhere -- final height only, vs.\ final height and growth speed.
Simpler version wins every time, a small but consistent gap, across four separate checks
(Table~\ref{tab:pinn-conditions}).
One candidate reason to prefer the two-parameter version -- converting its growth-speed number
into a yield class foresters already use -- did not hold up: a quirk in that formula, not
something this model found. Dropped.
Two real reasons remain. A fuller picture: final height and how fast a stand gets there, not
just one number. And a safer one: the two-parameter version never produces an implausible
height, where the simpler version occasionally does (Table~\ref{tab:plausibility-comparison}).
Checked why the extra parameter costs accuracy. The shared correction network works
consistently harder in the two-parameter version -- roughly double or more, in every one of the
5 folds checked (Table~\ref{tab:trunk-residual}). Growth speed does not fit cleanly by itself; something else
has to compensate. The two parameters do move together sensibly ($r=0.43$), though not
proportionally (Figure~\ref{fig:ymax-k-scatter}): $k$ deviation is almost entirely positive
while $y_{max}$ deviation skews negative, the same one-sided routing already seen at the
compartment level (Figure~\ref{fig:ymax-k-deviation-maps}) -- not fitting independently to
noise, but not a simple diagonal relationship either. Quantified directly, pooled across all 5
folds: 76.7\% of plots keep $y_{max,i}$ within $\pm$1m of population, while 92.1\% of plots get a
moderately-or-much-faster $k_i$ (Appendix~\ref{app:results-q3-figures}, Figure~\ref{fig:pinn-param-distribution}).
