\paragraph*{Trees still win on raw accuracy.}
XGBoost most accurate. DNN close behind. PINN and PINN-$k$ both trail.
Four possible reasons checked. All ruled out. Feature interactions. Compartment count.
Network size. Unfamiliar terrain. None explain the gap.
Training settings checked again this session. Same result. Gap stands.
Real question: does PINN still earn its place, despite losing here?

\paragraph*{The personalisation is real, not one hidden variable.}
Checked whether one terrain feature secretly drives the prediction. Same problem Q2 found in
a different model.
Shuffled each feature, one at a time. Watched how much the prediction moved.
No single feature dominates. Top feature: wind exposure. Only 15\% of the total.
All eleven features matter, roughly 5\% to 15\% each.
Q2's model leaned on one variable for 80--90\% of its signal. This model does not.
One fold checked so far. Not yet repeated on others.

\paragraph*{Environment helps a lot -- once. More does not help further.}
Tested PINN with no environment information at all. Then three lists of features, small to
large.
Zero environment info: $R^2$ 0.573, both versions.
Add any environment info: jumps to about 0.62--0.63. A real, solid gain.
Add a bigger list on top: no further gain. Slightly hurts the simpler PINN version.
A well-chosen list beats a big one.
This fixes a broken earlier test. The old code showed no difference at all. That was a bug,
not a real result.
One caveat: the medium list is not proven best for the two-parameter version. The small list
scores close to the same, within normal noise.
Checked why the big list does not help. Its extra features are not ignored. They get more
attention than their fair share. They just do not carry over to new stands as well.

\paragraph*{PINN's biggest flaw traces to one place -- and it is not a guessing problem.}
On average, PINN predicts a slightly taller ceiling than the shared curve. About 3\,m taller.
Most plots lean this way. Not a rare quirk.
A small number go further. Predictions no real tree reaches. Under 5\,m, or over 70\,m.
70\,m looked like a hard line. It is not. Heights taper smoothly just below it. So we looked
wider: the 40 most-inflated plots overall, not just the ones past the line.
31 of those 40 sit in one compartment. One place, out of hundreds in the forest.
Two smaller compartments show the same pattern, on a smaller scale.

Two explanations tested. One ruled out, one ruled out decisively.
First: does the model just misread unusual land? Checked directly. Did not hold up on repeat
folds. Dropped.
Second: is this a place the model never learned properly? Checked five ways. Trained on this
compartment's own real data in four of the five. Prediction stayed high every time. Highest of
all five when the model had seen every row of its data. Not a guessing problem.

What is left: a real, unusually strong site. Or a fault in how that one place's heights were
recorded. Both still open. The plain DNN also does slightly worse here, about a fifth worse
than elsewhere -- consistent with something genuinely unusual about the place itself, not just
a PINN quirk.

\paragraph*{The simpler PINN wins on accuracy. The two-parameter version buys something else.}
Compared the two versions everywhere. One predicts final height only. One predicts final
height and growth speed.
Simpler version wins every time. Small gap, about 0.01 to 0.02. Same result across four
separate checks.
Tried one reason to prefer the two-parameter version anyway: convert its growth-speed number
into a yield class foresters already use. Did not hold up. An old quirk in that formula, not
something this model found. Dropped.
The real reason to still want it: a fuller picture. Final height, and how fast a stand gets
there. Not just one number.
Checked why the extra parameter costs accuracy. The shared correction network works two to
three times harder in the two-parameter version. Gets worse across folds, not better. Growth
speed does not fit cleanly by itself. Something else has to compensate.
