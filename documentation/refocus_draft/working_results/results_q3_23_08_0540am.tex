\subsection{Discussion}

% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
% 1. PIVOT (folded-in, NOT its own section/heading) -- unchanged.
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------

\paragraph*{Trees still win on raw accuracy.}
XGBoost most accurate, DNN close behind, PINN and PINN-$k$ both trail
(Table~\ref{tab:results-q3}).
Four possible reasons checked for DNN's gap to XGBoost. All ruled out: feature interactions,
compartment count, network size, unfamiliar terrain.
Training settings checked again this session, at the correct batch size. Same result. Gap
stands.
Real question: does PINN still earn its place, despite losing here?

% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
% 2. IS PINN'S INTERPRETABILITY GENUINE? -- unchanged shape (2a real -> 2b driven by real
% signal -> 2c honest about where it breaks), but 2c is substantially rewritten.
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------

% --- 2a. The personalisation is real, not one variable in disguise -- unchanged ---

\paragraph*{The personalisation is real, not one hidden variable.}
Checked whether one terrain feature secretly drives the prediction. Same problem Q2 found in
a different model.
Shuffled each feature, one at a time, ten repeats each. Watched how much the prediction moved
(Figure~\ref{fig:terrain-importance}).
No single feature dominates. Top feature, wind exposure, carries 15.2\% of the total. All
eleven span 4.7\%--15.2\%.
Q2's model (GNNWR) leaned on one variable, CanopyCover, for 80--90\% of its signal. This model
does not.
One fold checked so far. Not yet repeated on others -- flagged, not hidden.

% --- 2b. The thing driving it is real (environment sweep) -- unchanged ---

\paragraph*{Environment helps a lot -- once. More does not help further.}
Tested PINN with no environment information, then three feature lists, small to large. Five-
fold CV throughout (Table~\ref{tab:pinn-conditions}, Figure~\ref{fig:env-sweep}).
Zero environment info to a small list: a real, solid jump, several times the fold-to-fold
noise floor.
A bigger list on top of that: no further gain. This fixes a broken earlier test -- the pre-fix
code showed no difference between having environment information or not at all, a bug, not a
real result.
Checked properly whether the medium list (the one used everywhere else in this chapter) is
actually the best choice, not just the highest number. Paired the three lists fold by fold,
since they share the same five splits -- the correct test, not just eyeballing the error bars.
Result: the three lists cannot be told apart. Any consistent edge for the medium list is
smaller than fold-to-fold noise, for both model versions.
So the honest claim is narrower than it first looked: some environment clearly beats none.
Which exact list, once you have one, does not matter on this evidence -- the medium list is
kept as the working choice because everything else in the chapter is built on it, not because
it was shown to be the best of the three.

% --- 2c. REWRITTEN: the personalisation tells a real, two-sided story ---

\paragraph*{Two flagship examples, picked by a fixed rule -- not by eye.}
The deviation map (Figure~\ref{fig:ymax-deviation-map}) has many compartments darker than the
two examples used below, on both the red and the blue side. A fair question: why these two?
Rule, fixed before picking, not after: only compartments with 50+ pooled held-out plots count.
Small compartments (some as few as 7-20 plots) can look extreme from one or two outlier plots
alone -- not a real site pattern (Table~\ref{tab:productivity-top5}).
Two different questions, two different winners. "Which single plot is most extreme?" -- 1129,
rank 1 of 178 reliable compartments, on one plot alone (+25.8\,m). "Which compartment has the
biggest average shift, and is also the most extreme single case?" -- 2021, rank 1 of 178 on
both counts (Table~\ref{tab:productivity-flagships}).
1129 is not the top compartment by average -- it ranks 10th. Compartments that look darker on
the map mostly fail the 50-plot bar. 2021 has no such ambiguity: it wins outright on both
rankings.
Checked whether 1129 and 2021 show up the same way in PINN-$k$'s growth-rate parameter, not
just plain PINN's ceiling -- they do not, once pooled across all 5 folds rather than fold 0
alone (an earlier read of fold-0-only data suggested 1129 did; correcting that here). PINN-$k$'s
own flagship, by growth rate, is a different compartment (2057 -- see below, it turns out to
matter for a different reason).
Checked against the forestry service's own yield-class records -- recorded independently,
never seen by either model. 2021 and the wider under-productive top 5 agree with a low yield
class in 4 of 5 cases. The over-productive top 5 agree in only 3 of 5 -- see below.
Checked this is not just two cherry-picked compartments: a real, significant correlation holds
across the whole test set, not just the extremes (Table~\ref{tab:yieldclass-correlation}).
Modest, not dominant -- yield class explains part of the picture, not most of it. Terrain
(2a) is still doing most of the work.

\paragraph*{The over-productive mismatch has a structure, not just noise.}
Checked what splits the two disagreeing compartments (2057, 1027) from the three agreeing ones
(1130, 2130, 1122). Compartment by compartment, not just the two group averages.
Clean split, no exceptions. Disagreeing pair: old (60, 72 years), wind-exposed (windward index
+15, +11). Agreeing three: young (25-35 years), sheltered (-9 to 0).
Not a new mystery -- ties to two things already found. Old stands: exactly where both PINN
versions extrapolate worst (Table~\ref{tab:age-height-error}). Wind exposure: the single
biggest terrain input to the model (Figure~\ref{fig:terrain-importance}, 15.2\%).
Reading: in old, windy stands, part of the "over-productive" push may be extrapolation past the
model's well-observed range -- not a clean productivity signal.
Yield class is a fixed site rating. Does not move with age. A mismatch there is expected for
this profile, not a sign the wider story is unreliable.
Independent check, found afterwards, not built in: 2057 is also PINN-$k$'s own top compartment
for growth rate, pooled across all 5 folds -- rank 1 of 178 by both average and single most
extreme plot. Two models, two different parameters, agree this compartment is unusual.
Reading: the age/wind-exposure signal is real and robust across both models -- it just does not
happen to be the same signal yield class captures.
Caveat: only 5 compartments checked for the age/exposure split. Not yet tested at scale.

\paragraph*{The two PINN versions differ in how safely they express the same signal.}
Plain PINN sometimes pushes the signal too far -- a small number of plots reach a height no
real tree does. PINN-$k$ never does this, on 58,073 pooled plots, not just one fold
(Table~\ref{tab:plausibility-comparison}, Figure~\ref{fig:plausibility-distribution}).
Plain PINN has only one outlet for this signal, the ceiling itself. PINN-$k$ has two, and
mostly uses growth rate instead, leaving the ceiling close to the shared curve.
Checked this is not a held-out generalisation problem, the same way as before: trained on the
compartment's own real data in four of five folds, the inflated prediction did not shrink --
if anything it grew (Table~\ref{tab:compartment1129-crossfold}).
What is left unresolved: a real, unusually strong or weak site, or a fault in how some of
these places' heights were recorded. Both still open, though the yield-class match makes the
first explanation more likely than it looked before.

\paragraph*{The same weakness still shows up at the extremes of age and height.}
Checked whether mistakes cluster by tree age or height, across all three models, same
held-out rows (Table~\ref{tab:age-height-error}).
Young and mid-range ages: all three models close, sometimes the simpler PINN slightly ahead.
Oldest ages and shortest stands: DNN clearly best, PINN-$k$ the worst of the three.
Not resolved by anything above. A personal curve, fit mostly to well-observed ages, does not
stretch well to the extremes -- the same mechanism as compartment 1129's implausible cases,
now shown as a general pattern, not fixed by having a second parameter available.

% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
% 3. PLAIN PINN VS. PINN-K: THE RECOMMENDATION -- gains a new safety point.
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------

\paragraph*{The simpler PINN wins on accuracy. The two-parameter version buys a fuller,
and safer, picture.}
Compared the two versions everywhere -- final height only, vs.\ final height and growth speed.
Simpler version wins every time, a small but consistent gap, across four separate checks
(Table~\ref{tab:pinn-conditions}).
Tried one reason to prefer the two-parameter version anyway: convert its growth-speed number
into a yield class foresters already use. Did not hold up, an old quirk in that formula, not
something this model found. Dropped.
Two real reasons remain. A fuller picture: final height and how fast a stand gets there, not
just one number. And a safer one: the two-parameter version never produces an implausible
height, where the simpler version occasionally does (Table~\ref{tab:plausibility-comparison}).
Checked why the extra parameter costs accuracy. The shared correction network works
increasingly harder in the two-parameter version, across every fold checked
(Table~\ref{tab:trunk-residual}). Growth speed does not fit cleanly by itself; something else
has to compensate. The two parameters do move together sensibly, though
(Figure~\ref{fig:ymax-k-scatter}) -- not fitting independently to noise.

\paragraph*{The settings were checked, not guessed.}
Learning rate, batch size, and physics-weighting strength each tested against other values
(Table~\ref{tab:pinn-conditions}). None beat what was already reported.
Plain PINN still wins under every one. Not a one-setting fluke.

\paragraph*{Appendix: why the broadest feature list looked worse -- and why that is a weak
finding, not a strong one.}
Checked, before the paired fold-by-fold test above (2b) softened the headline claim, why the
broadest environment list (Set4) seemed to underperform the medium list (Set3).
Set4's extra features are not ignored by the model -- they draw more than their fair share of
permutation importance -- they just do not repay that attention in held-out accuracy as well as
the medium list's more curated features.
Caveat now folded in: the Set3-vs-Set4 gap this was explaining is itself within fold-to-fold
noise for plain PINN (weak, non-significant tendency, Table~\ref{tab:pinn-conditions}), and
absent entirely for PINN-$k$. So this is a plausible explanation for a small, uncertain effect,
not an explanation for a solid one -- kept here as a secondary note, not promoted to the main
argument.
